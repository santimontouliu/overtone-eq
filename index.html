How to read the waveform

What happens moment by moment?

The horizontal axis is time in milliseconds. The vertical axis is the instantaneous, signed signal amplitude. Above and below zero are opposite directions of the oscillation—not positive and negative loudness.

Every frequency component contributes a sine wave. At each instant, those values add. Components can reinforce or cancel each other, making peaks, troughs, and the overall shape.

After EQ: boosting an existing component gives its oscillation more weight in the sum; cutting it gives it less. Emphasizing higher harmonics can add fine ripples or sharpen transitions, while removing them can smooth the signal. The exact shape also depends on relative phase.

Watch the scale: both traces use the same automatic vertical scale. A louder signal can make the axis expand instead of looking taller. Compare the original and processed traces and read the axis labels; do not compare pixel height alone across different settings.

How to read the spectrum

Which frequencies are present?

The horizontal axis is frequency in hertz, on a logarithmic scale: equal spacing means equal frequency ratios. Moving from 100 Hz to 1 kHz spans the same distance as moving from 1 kHz to 10 kHz.

Each vertical line marks one frequency present in the synthesized signal. Its top shows that component’s peak amplitude in decibels, referenced to amplitude 1. For example, 0 dB means amplitude 1; −6.02 dB means about 0.5; +6.02 dB means about 2. Negative dB means smaller than the reference, not a negative oscillation.

Muted violet = original. Amber = after EQ. Paired lines are shifted a few pixels apart so you can compare them; that tiny visual offset is not a pitch change. Boosting EQ raises an affected amber line, cutting lowers it, and its frequency stays fixed.

This is a calculated line spectrum of the demo tones, not a music recording, microphone measurement, or live FFT. The display spans −60 to +24 dB. Silence has no line; components below the floor are not resolved. The spectrum shows magnitude, not phase, so different waveforms can share the same spectrum.

How EQ changes the signal

Why a slider is not a spectrum bar

An EQ slider sets a gain in dB around its labeled center frequency. It does not set that frequency’s absolute amplitude. The bell-shaped band also affects nearby frequencies, and bands overlap. The magenta response curve shows their combined effect.

Lout(f)=Lin(f)+Gtotal(f)L_{\mathrm{out}}(f)=L_{\mathrm{in}}(f)+G_{\mathrm{total}}(f)Lout​(f)=Lin​(f)+Gtotal​(f)

For a nonzero component, its output level is its input level plus the total EQ gain at that frequency. A line starting at −12 dB with +6 dB of EQ ends at −6 dB. If the source has no component there, a boost cannot create one.

The causal chain: change a band → change the response at nearby frequencies → change their amplitudes and phases → add the components again → get a new waveform. The EQ response is a property of the filter; the spectrum is a property of the signal.

Why do equalizers use genre names?

Names such as Rock, Jazz, and Soul are listening shortcuts: they suggest a tonal balance that a preset designer associates with that style. They do not identify a special kind of sound wave, and they do not turn one genre into another.

There is no universal Rock or Jazz curve. Different players and manufacturers can use different gains, band frequencies, and filter widths under the same name. The curves in Overtone are our own teaching examples, not copies of a particular device.

Choose a preset by what you hear, not just the track’s genre. The recording, headphones, speakers, room, and your preference all matter. Start from Flat, compare with bypass, and use small adjustments.

Try this here: select Sawtooth-ish, choose a listening mode, and toggle bypass. A pure sine has only one frequency, so most EQ differences will mainly change its level; a harmonic-rich signal makes changes in timbre easier to hear. This lab synthesizes tones, not rock or jazz recordings.

Model notes, plot conventions & further reading

The waveform and line spectrum are calculated from the same sinusoidal model used for playback; they are not microphone measurements or a live FFT. The animation advances slowly for readability, while the time labels show the actual signal window. “Freeze plots” pauses only the visual time offset. Both waveform traces share one automatic scale. At wide time windows, the plot preserves each screen pixel’s minimum and maximum samples to show the envelope without hiding narrow peaks.

Playback starts only when you press Listen. Output volume is separate from the scientific plots. A conservative automatic headroom trim bounds the sum of component amplitudes before playback; it is shared between original and EQ paths for a useful bypass comparison. It can reduce overall playback level when you boost bands. Plots always show the untrimmed relative signal. Start with a low device volume.

Audio changes use a brief crossfade. Harmonic phases and the second-tone phase are relative to a common source start. No input recording, upload, account, or network connection is needed.
